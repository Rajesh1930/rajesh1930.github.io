Sheet 6 · High-speed CFD

Double wedge at Mach 3 and Mach 10

A 2D double-wedge body at 4° angle of attack, simulated at supersonic (Mach 3) and hypersonic (Mach 10) speeds to compare the shock structure.

  • Built the geometry and a structured quad mesh, refined around the body, in Ansys 2022 R1.
  • Density-based Fluent solver with air as an ideal gas.
  • Compared static pressure, total pressure, velocity, Mach number and vorticity between the two cases.
1.05 MPaPeak static pressure, Mach 3
35.1 MPaPeak static pressure, Mach 10
What it showed: a detached bow shock forms in both cases. At Mach 10 it sits closer to the body and is swept back more sharply, and the pressure behind it is about 30 times higher.

Vehicle benchmarking study

The brief: recommend a car for a family of five in Bangalore with a budget of ₹8–10 lakh on road. It had to handle city commuting and a monthly 500 km trip to Madurai, and the buyer preferred a bigger car.

I compared five vehicles on safety (airbags), fuel economy, boot space, seating and price. Benchmarking means studying competitors' strengths and weaknesses so a new design improves where theirs falls short.

Week 1 · Body protection

Underbody coating study

A short research report on underbody coating: the protective layer sprayed onto the underside of a car to resist corrosion, stone chips and road salt. It's often confused with rust-proofing, which treats internal cavities rather than the exposed underbody.

I compared the four main coating types on how they're applied, how durable they are and where each fits. Coating improves corrosion life and reduces road noise, at the cost of extra application expense.

TypeStrengthLimitation
RubberisedEasy to apply; soft finish that damps noiseSofter, less chip-resistant
PolyurethaneSeeps into seams and bonds to bare metalNeeds rust sanded off first
Wax / paraffinCheapest and quickest; flows into seamsNever hardens; reapply yearly
Asphalt-basedMost durable; suits trucks and heavy useLong cure; not paintable

Fender design

Fender wheel-arch clearance check (European standard)

Checked a front fender against the European wheel-arch coverage rule in Siemens NX. I set up reference lines from the wheel centre: vertical (SA), 30° (SB), 50° (SC), a line to a point 150 mm above the centre line (SE), and the midpoint between SC and SE (SD). Then I measured the clearance L between the tyre and the arch at each one, against a 10 mm minimum.

Result: does not comply. Only SB passes (10.57 mm). SA, SC, SD and SE fall between 8.2 and 9.9 mm, so the arch edge needs extending at those positions. The report also covers the Japanese (≥ 5 mm at 30° and 50°) and American (≥ 5 mm every 10°) versions of the check.
